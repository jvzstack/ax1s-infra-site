Reserved Instances vs. Savings Plans: which fits your workload?

Tom Prager·2 Jul 2026·6 min read

Two routes to the same discount

As soon as a workload runs predictably and continuously, you leave money on the table by paying everything at the on-demand rate. Cloud providers reward commitment with substantial discounts, generally between 20 and 60 percent, depending on term and type of commitment. The two best-known routes are Reserved Instances (RIs) and Savings Plans. They look alike, but the differences determine in practice how much flexibility you retain.

Reserved Instances: maximum discount, less flexibility

With a Reserved Instance you commit to a specific instance type, in a specific region, for a period of usually one or three years. The discount is generally the highest, especially with an upfront payment. The flip side: if your architecture changes, or you switch to a different instance type, the reservation no longer matches and you still miss out on the return.

Savings Plans: slightly lower discount, much more room to move

A Savings Plan works differently: you commit to a fixed amount of usage per hour, regardless of which instance type, family or even (with some providers) region you use. That makes the instrument resilient to architectural changes. The discount is generally slightly lower than with an RI of the same term, but the risk of "wasted" commitment is considerably smaller.

Which fits your situation?

  • Stable, long-unchanged workload on a fixed instance type? A Reserved Instance delivers the highest discount.
  • Growing or changing infrastructure, migrations planned, or multiple teams making their own instance choices? Go for a Savings Plan.
  • In doubt? Start with a Savings Plan on your most predictable base usage and build it out gradually.

The biggest pitfall: overcommitment

The biggest mistake we see is not choosing the wrong instrument, but committing to too much volume. Always calculate on the basis of your minimum, structural usage over recent months, not on your peak load. Anything above that you simply leave running on on-demand.

Rule of thumb: never commit to more than 70 to 80 percent of your average usage. That way you keep room for seasonal fluctuations and growth, without missing the discount on your base usage.

Combining is possible, and often optimal

In practice a layered approach works best: Savings Plans for the flexible base, supplemented with targeted Reserved Instances on the workloads that demonstrably stay stable for years. That does require ongoing monitoring: commitments are not a one-off decision, but an instrument you need to recalibrate periodically as your infrastructure changes.
