Case: 23% savings in a focused engagement

Jelle van Zantwijk·22 May 2026·5 min read

The starting point

A fast-growing SaaS scale-up approached us with a familiar problem: the cloud bill was growing faster than revenue. Nobody could say exactly why: there was no clear picture of which team used what, and optimisation had never been a structural part of engineering. The pressure to scale had simply taken priority over cost control.

Phase 1: analysis

We began with a full inventory of cloud usage over the previous three months, combined with a tagging audit. It soon turned out that a substantial part of the infrastructure was not labelled consistently, which had made cost analysis per team impossible until then. In parallel we mapped the largest cost items: compute, storage and data transfer, in that order.

Phase 2: quick wins

While the broader analysis continued, we immediately implemented the first improvements:

  • Cleaning up unused storage volumes and outdated snapshots
  • Automatically shutting down test and staging environments outside office hours
  • Rightsizing the most oversized production instances

Phase 3: structural optimisation

With cleaner and more transparent usage, we could then tackle the structural layer: a Savings Plan aligned with the demonstrably stable base usage, and a tagging standard that was automatically enforced from then on when creating new resources. A monthly cost overview per team was also set up, so that ownership didn't rest with one person but was carried organisation-wide.

Result: an average saving of 23% on monthly cloud and compute spend, achieved in a focused engagement from the first intake, with no impact on performance or scalability.

What makes this case typical

This outcome doesn't stand alone: the pattern (quick wins in the first weeks, followed by structural optimisation that keeps working in the long term) we see in almost every engagement. The order matters more than the individual measures: without visibility and clean-up first, structural optimisation is hard to sustain.
