This AI project looked cheap, until the bill arrived

Tom Prager·25 Aug 2026·3 min read

Month 1: a small pilot

A team gets the green light for a pilot: an AI model that answers customer questions automatically. Test budget: small. Scope: a hundred conversations a day, purely for validation.

The results are good. Customers respond positively, the team is enthusiastic, and the pilot's costs barely stand out in the monthly invoice. Everyone is happy. The idea is approved for roll-out to the entire customer service department.

Month 2: roll-out without recalculation

The model goes live for all incoming conversations. Nobody recalculates upfront what that means; the pilot worked well, so why would scaling up be any different from more of the same?

The number of conversations rises from a hundred to ten thousand a day. Not gradually, but immediately, because that was the whole point of the roll-out.

Month 3: the bill

The first full invoice comes in. Nobody had a number in mind to compare it against, so at first the amount doesn't even stand out. Only when finance puts it next to the previous month does it become clear: the costs are not a hundred times, but almost three hundred times higher than during the pilot.

Only then does the reason become visible. The pilot ran on a test environment with a different pricing model. Production has different rates, and nobody had calculated that difference upfront.

The model still works well. Customers are still satisfied. But the cost per conversation turns out to be three times higher than the business case had foreseen, and that difference could have been calculated from day one, if someone had asked.

The lesson isn't in the technology, but in the order. A pilot proves that something works. A calculation at full scale proves that it stays profitable. Those are two different questions, and usually only the first one is asked.
