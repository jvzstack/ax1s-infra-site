AI budget exceeded? You're not the only one

Tom Prager·10 Aug 2026·4 min read

How a pilot grows into a cost item nobody saw coming

The pattern is familiar by now. A pilot with a few LLM calls grows within a few months into ten teams running on the same model. Nobody let that happen on purpose: it just happened, because nobody was tracking it. There was no moment when someone consciously decided to triple the budget. There was only a series of small, logical decisions that together produced a very different bill.

Where it goes wrong

At AX1S we see four recurring causes when an AI budget gets out of hand.

  • Inference isn't seen as a cost item, but as "just an API call". Until the invoice is three times as high as last month, and nobody can pinpoint which team or feature is responsible.
  • Engineering builds, finance pays. Who causes the costs and who is responsible for them are rarely the same person. Without clear cost allocation, nobody feels ownership of the usage.
  • GPU capacity is reserved upfront, not evaluated afterwards. Overcapacity for AI workloads is more expensive than in regular cloud environments, and often only becomes apparent when it's already too late.
  • Nobody asks upfront: what will this model cost when scaled to production? A pilot with 50 users behaves completely differently financially from a production environment with 5,000 users, but that calculation is rarely made in advance.

Do you want to make that calculation upfront? Our AI Cost Calculator shows directly, based on model class, volume and tokens, what a chosen set-up will cost, including the breakdown and a growth projection to production scale.

The figures confirm the pattern: 98% of FinOps practitioners now name AI cost management as a top priority, compared with only a fraction of that two years ago. That is no coincidence. It is an industry catching up on a problem that has existed for a long time.

The good news: each of these four causes can be recognised and fixed as soon as you start looking at them. Begin with the question most organisations can't yet answer: do you know exactly what your latest AI feature costs in inference per 1,000 users? If not, that is precisely where it goes wrong.
